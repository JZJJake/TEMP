🧠 The Physics Behind the Thermal Numbers
1. Silicon Carbide (SiC) Baseline: 400 W/m·K
Phonon Scattering: SiC conducts heat entirely through lattice vibrations (phonons). Its thermal conductivity is restricted by the mass difference between Silicon and Carbon atoms, which enhances intrinsic phonon-phonon (Umklapp) scattering.
Limitations: While 400 W/m·K is excellent compared to Silicon (~148 W/m·K), it cannot dissipate heat fast enough when GaN HEMTs are driven to extreme power densities (>10 W/mm), creating severe local hotspots.
2. Polycrystalline Diamond (PCD): 1000–1800 W/m·K
The Nucleation Bottleneck: PCD is typically grown directly on GaN using Chemical Vapor Deposition (CVD). Near the initial seed layer (the nucleation zone), the diamond crystals are extremely small, disordered, and filled with amorphous carbon defects. [9, 10, 11] 
Thermal Gradient: At the interface where heat extraction matters most, the thermal conductivity can drop as low as 100–300 W/m·K. It only reaches its high bulk values (up to 1800 W/m·K) after growing tens of microns thick and forming larger crystal grains. This spatial discrepancy creates a high Thermal Boundary Resistance (TBR).[12] 
3. Single-Crystal Diamond (SCD): 2200 W/m·K [13, 14] 
Pure Phonon Transport: Because it consists of a flawless, rigid, three-dimensional covalent carbon lattice with ultra-light atomic mass, phonons travel virtually unimpeded. This allows it to hit the theoretical maximum of 2200 W/m·K. [15] 
Uniform Extraction: Unlike PCD, SCD maintains this maximum thermal conductivity right up to the interface. There is no low-conductivity nucleation layer. [16]

1. The Physics of $400^{\circ}\text{C}$ Thermal Degradation
The 2DHG on an H-diamond surface does not rely on bulk chemical doping. Instead, it works via surface transfer doping, which creates a critical vulnerability to thermal stress: [2, 5, 6, 7] 
  UNPASSIVATED (Catastrophic Loss at 400°C)
  [ Atmospheric Adsorbates / H2O + CO2 ]  <─── Desorbs completely at ~400°C
  ──────────────────────────────────────
  [ C–H Dipole Layer ]                    <─── Individual H-atoms dissociate above 400K-500K
  ======================================
  [ Diamond Valence Band (Holes) ]        <─── 2DHG vanishes; sheet resistance spikes
Atmospheric Adsorbate Desorption: The C-H dipole layer reduces electron affinity, allowing airborne molecules ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$, $\text{CO}_2$) to accept electrons from the diamond valence band. At temperatures near $400^{\circ}\text{C}$, these volatile adsorbates desorb completely. Without these electron acceptors, the 2DHG channel collapses, causing sheet resistance to spike by orders of magnitude. [2, 3, 8, 9] 
C–H Bond Dissociation: While a pure C–H bond on diamond is strong in a vacuum up to $\sim 700^{\circ}\text{C}$, the presence of trace oxygen at $400^{\circ}\text{C}$ triggers surface oxidation. Individual hydrogen atoms dissociate, replacing the p-type C–H terminals with insulating C–O or C=O bonds, permanently destroying the transistor's transport properties. [9, 10] 

2. Passivation Strategies and the $400^{\circ}\text{C}$ Bottleneck
To build a reliable power device, the H-diamond surface must be sealed using a thin-film dielectric passivation layer—typically Aluminum Oxide ($\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$) deposited via Atomic Layer Deposition (ALD). However, the choice of ALD processing parameters determines whether the device survives or degrades at $400^{\circ}\text{C}$. [1, 8, 11, 12] 
❌ Low-Temperature ALD Passivation ($200^{\circ}\text{C} - 300^{\circ}\text{C}$)
The Mechanism: Depositing $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$ at low temperatures protects the pre-existing surface adsorbates, preserving excellent room-temperature drain currents ($I_{DS}$). [5, 8, 12, 13] 
The Failure at $400^{\circ}\text{C}$: These films have lower density and contain trapped moisture or chemical precursors. When heated toward $400^{\circ}\text{C}$ during device operation, the underlying adsorbates and structural gases outgas. This builds up pressure beneath the dielectric, causing bubble-like peeling patterns or pinhole ruptures in the oxide, leading to catastrophic gate leakage and device failure. [3, 11] 
High-Temperature ALD Passivation ($450^{\circ}\text{C}$) [1] 
The Mechanism: Pioneered by groups like Kawarada et al., this technique bypasses volatile air adsorbates entirely. It deposits dense $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$ directly at $450^{\circ}\text{C}$ using $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ as an oxidant. [1, 5, 11, 14, 15] 
Why it Survives: The high processing temperature forces the formation of a pristine $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3/\text{H-diamond}$ interface. Unoccupied energy states deep inside the high-density oxide act as permanent, solid-state electron acceptors. Because these fixed negative charges substitute for atmospheric moisture, the 2DHG layer remains stable and functional up to $500^{\circ}\text{C}$ in open air without structural degradation. [1, 11, 14, 16]

While p-type doping via boron is highly efficient, phosphorus-doped diamond suffers from severe fundamental constraints. Despite critical lab breakthroughs, achieving high room-temperature (RT) carrier concentrations remains a largely unsolved problem due to a combination of rigid physics, structural mismatches, and structural compensation. [1, 2] 

🧱 1. The Fundamental Physics Bottleneck: Deep Donor Level
The primary reason n-type diamond remains "unsolved" for conventional ambient electronics is the deep activation energy of phosphorus. [3] 
The 0.6 eV Barrier: Substitutional phosphorus creates a donor energy level ~0.60 eV below the conduction band.[4, 5] 
The Ionization Problem: Because the thermal energy at room temperature ($k_B T$) is only ~26 meV, less than 0.1% of incorporated phosphorus atoms actually ionize to release a free conduction electron at 300 K.
The Temperature Paradox: To get reasonable carrier concentrations and mobility, devices must be heated to $250^{\circ}\text{C} - 300^{\circ}\text{C}$. While excellent for niche high-temperature sensors, it makes standard RT computing unviable. [6] 

💥 2. The Size Mismatch & Incorporation Challenge
Carbon has a tiny covalent radius, making it extremely hostile to larger dopant atoms. [7, 8] 
Lattice Distortion: The covalent radius of Phosphorus (1.10 Å) is significantly larger than Carbon (0.77 Å). Forcing a phosphorus atom into a substitutional diamond site creates massive localized lattice strain and has an incredibly high formation energy of +6.65 eV. [8, 9, 10] 
The Crystal Orientation Dilemma: Efficient P-incorporation only happens easily on the (111) crystallographic orientation during Chemical Vapor Deposition (CVD). However, (111) diamond grows incredibly slowly and is highly prone to stacking faults and defects. Conversely, the highly stable, electronic-grade (100) orientationexhibits a P-incorporation efficiency that is 100× lower. [4, 11, 12, 13, 14] 

⚖️ 3. The Self-Compensation Phenomenon
When engineers attempt to force high quantities of phosphorus into the gas phase during Microwave Plasma CVD (MPCVD) to override the ionization limit, the diamond lattice reacts to alleviate stress: [15, 16] 
Defect Generation: Heavy phosphorus doping shifts the Fermi level and creates favorable conditions for spontaneous vacancy formation (carbon vacancies, $V_C$) and hydrogen-defect complexes. [2] 
Carrier Compensation: These vacancy defects act as deep acceptors (p-type traps). They instantly capture the few free electrons generated by the phosphorus donors, locking the net carrier concentration down near zero and rendering the material semi-insulating. [17, 18]
